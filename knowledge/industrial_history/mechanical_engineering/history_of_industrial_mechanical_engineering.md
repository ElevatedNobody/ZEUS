## Machine design becomes an engineering discipline {claim: historical_fact}
The nineteenth century established mechanics of materials, kinematics and
thermodynamics as the basis for machine design. Rankine, Reynolds, Stodola and
Timoshenko turned rules of thumb into calculable relationships between load, stress and
deflection.

## Fatigue {claim: historical_fact}
August Wohler's railway axle testing in the 1860s established that a component can fail
under repeated loads far below its static strength, and produced the stress-life curve
still used today. Fatigue remains the single most common mechanical failure mode in
rotating and cyclically loaded equipment.

## Fracture mechanics {claim: historical_fact}
Griffith's work on brittle fracture in 1920 and the later development of linear elastic
fracture mechanics by Irwin explained why large structures fail from small flaws. The
Liberty ship fractures of the 1940s made the practical importance unmistakable and led
to toughness requirements in materials specifications.

## Standardisation of pressure equipment {claim: historical_fact}
After a series of catastrophic boiler explosions, notably the Grover Shoe Factory
disaster in Brockton, Massachusetts in 1905, Massachusetts introduced boiler rules and
the ASME issued the first Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code in 1914-1915. Almost every
modern vessel, boiler and piping code descends from that lineage.

## Balancing, alignment and vibration {claim: historical_fact}
As machines got faster, rotor dynamics became a design constraint. Field balancing,
precision shaft alignment and vibration acceptance criteria became routine industrial
practice through the mid twentieth century, later codified in ISO standards for
vibration evaluation of machines.

## Tribology and lubrication {claim: historical_fact}
The term tribology was coined in the 1966 Jost Report in the United Kingdom, which
estimated large national economic losses from friction and wear. Lubrication moved from
craft to engineering: film thickness, viscosity selection, contamination control and
oil analysis.

## Failure is usually a system property {claim: engineering_principle}
Most mechanical failures in process plants are not material defects. They are
misalignment, unbalance, resonance, inadequate support, poor lubrication, operation off
the design point, or repeated transient loading. Root cause analysis that stops at "the
bearing failed" has stopped one step too early.

## Design margin and its misuse {claim: engineering_principle}
Codes provide margin against uncertainty in loads, materials and fabrication. Margin is
not a licence to operate beyond design: consuming it deliberately - overpressure,
oversized impellers, higher temperature - removes the protection the code assumed.

## Materials selection {claim: engineering_principle}
Material choice balances strength, toughness, corrosion resistance, fabricability and
cost. In refining, most upgrades are driven by damage mechanisms rather than strength:
chrome-moly for high temperature hydrogen service, stainless for chloride-free
corrosive duty, and clad or overlaid vessels where only the wetted surface needs the
alloy.

## Current practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Modern mechanical practice pairs finite element analysis for stress and thermal
analysis with precision maintenance in the field - laser alignment, controlled bolt
tensioning, contamination-controlled lubrication - because analysis cannot compensate
for poor installation.
