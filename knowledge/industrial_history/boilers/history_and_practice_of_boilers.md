## Early boilers and their failures {claim: historical_fact}
Nineteenth-century industry ran on boilers that exploded with appalling regularity.
Wrought-iron shell boilers, poor water treatment, tampered safety valves and no
inspection regime combined to kill thousands. Boiler insurance companies and inspection
associations formed specifically to reduce these losses.

## The Grover Shoe Factory explosion and its consequence {claim: historical_fact}
The boiler explosion at the Grover Shoe Factory in Brockton, Massachusetts on 20 March
1905 destroyed the building and killed 58 people. Massachusetts introduced boiler rules,
and the ASME published the first Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code in 1914-1915. Modern
pressure equipment regulation worldwide descends from this response.

## Water-tube boilers {claim: historical_fact}
Babcock and Wilcox commercialised the water-tube boiler from 1867. Putting water inside
tubes and combustion gas outside allowed far higher pressures and steam rates safely,
and made the large utility and process boilers of the twentieth century possible.

## Heat recovery steam generators {claim: historical_fact}
As gas turbines entered industrial and power service, HRSGs recovering exhaust heat
became common, and cogeneration became standard practice on refinery and petrochemical
sites where both steam and power are needed.

## Indian regulation {claim: historical_fact}
In India, boilers are governed by the Indian Boilers Act of 1923 and the Indian Boiler
Regulations of 1950, administered by state boiler inspectorates, which require
registration, periodic inspection and certification of both boilers and welders.

## Water chemistry is boiler integrity {claim: engineering_principle}
Most boiler tube failures trace to water chemistry: dissolved oxygen causing pitting,
hardness causing scale that overheats the tube, and carryover causing superheater
deposits. Deaeration, chemical dosing, blowdown control and condensate polishing are
integrity controls, not chemistry housekeeping.

## Overheating and short-term failure {claim: engineering_principle}
A tube that loses cooling by scale, deposit or loss of circulation overheats within
minutes. The characteristic thin-lipped fishmouth rupture is the signature of short-term
overheating; thick-lipped rupture with creep voids indicates long-term overheating.

## Safety valves and controls {claim: engineering_principle}
A boiler's protection depends on safety valve capacity, low water level trips, flame
supervision and purge interlocks. Burner management system interlocks - purge before
light-off, flame proving, fuel trip on flame loss - exist because furnace explosions
follow accumulated unburnt fuel.

## Fired heaters in refineries {claim: engineering_principle}
Refinery fired heaters share boiler failure physics but with process fluid in the tubes.
Tube metal temperature limits, coke laydown, flame impingement, and hot spots set run
length. Loss of process flow with fire on is the classic path to tube rupture and heater
fire.

## Efficiency {claim: engineering_principle}
Boiler and heater efficiency is dominated by stack loss: excess air and stack
temperature. Oxygen trim, air preheat and correct burner maintenance typically deliver
more energy saving for less capital than any other single measure on a fired system.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice includes online tube skin thermocouples on critical heaters, infrared
survey of tube and refractory condition, formal burner management systems certified to a
safety integrity level, and statutory inspection intervals tracked with the same rigour
as process equipment.
