## Simple distillation {claim: historical_fact}
The first refineries were batch stills producing kerosene, with gasoline and heavy
residue treated as waste. Continuous pipe stills with fractionating towers replaced
batch operation in the early twentieth century and remain the front end of every
refinery today.

## Thermal cracking {claim: historical_fact}
William Burton's thermal cracking process was patented in 1913 for Standard Oil of
Indiana. By heating gas oil under pressure it roughly doubled gasoline yield from a
barrel of crude. Thermal processes survive today as visbreaking and delayed coking.

## Catalytic cracking {claim: historical_fact}
Eugene Houdry's fixed-bed catalytic cracking unit started up at Sun Oil's Paulsboro
refinery in 1936, producing higher octane gasoline than thermal cracking. The fixed
beds required frequent regeneration, which limited throughput.

## Fluid catalytic cracking {claim: historical_fact}
The first commercial fluid catalytic cracking unit, Model I, started at Standard Oil of
New Jersey's Baton Rouge refinery in 1942. Circulating powdered catalyst between a
reactor and a regenerator allowed continuous operation and continuous catalyst
regeneration. FCC remains the principal conversion unit in most fuels refineries.

## Catalytic reforming {claim: historical_fact}
UOP's Platforming process, commercialised in 1949, used a platinum catalyst to convert
low-octane naphtha into high-octane reformate and, as a by-product, hydrogen. That
hydrogen made widespread hydrotreating economically possible.

## Hydroprocessing {claim: historical_fact}
Hydrotreating and hydrocracking spread from the late 1950s onward, driven first by the
availability of reformer hydrogen and later by fuel sulphur regulation. Modern
ultra-low-sulphur diesel specifications are met almost entirely by hydrotreating over
cobalt-molybdenum or nickel-molybdenum catalysts.

## Environmental drivers {claim: historical_fact}
The removal of tetraethyl lead from gasoline from the 1970s, sulphur limits on fuels
from the 1990s and 2000s, and the IMO 2020 marine fuel sulphur cap each forced large
capital programmes: more reforming and isomerisation for octane, more hydrotreating and
more sulphur recovery capacity.

## The modern refinery configuration {claim: engineering_principle}
A fuels refinery is a sequence of separation, conversion and treatment. Crude
distillation separates by boiling range; vacuum distillation recovers gas oil from
residue without cracking it; conversion units (FCC, hydrocracker, coker) break heavy
molecules; treating units (hydrotreaters, amine, sour water stripper, sulphur recovery)
remove contaminants; blending assembles finished products to specification.

## Refinery margin and complexity {claim: engineering_principle}
Refinery value comes from upgrading cheap heavy or sour crude into light clean
products. The Nelson complexity index expresses how much conversion capability a
refinery has relative to its crude distillation capacity. Higher complexity means more
equipment, more hydrogen, more energy and more maintenance exposure.

## Refining equipment risk profile {claim: engineering_principle}
Refining concentrates hazards in a small number of equipment classes: fired heaters
(tube rupture and coking), FCC (high-temperature catalyst circulation and slide valve
integrity), hydroprocessing (high pressure hydrogen and HTHA), and hot piping systems
(sulphidation, creep, and corrosion under insulation).

## Current practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Refiners typically pursue reliability-driven turnaround intervals of four to six years,
integrity operating windows tied to corrosion mechanisms, advanced process control on
major units, and energy optimisation of the crude and vacuum heat integration network.
