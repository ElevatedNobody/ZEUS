## Scheduling methods {claim: historical_fact}
The Gantt chart dates from the 1910s. The Critical Path Method was developed by DuPont
and Remington Rand in 1957 for plant shutdown and construction planning, and PERT by the
United States Navy for the Polaris programme in 1958. Both are still the basis of
turnaround scheduling.

## Project management as a profession {claim: historical_fact}
The Project Management Institute was founded in 1969 and published the first PMBOK Guide
in 1987. Stage-gate processes for capital projects, notably front-end loading
methodologies used across oil and gas, formalised the finding that decisions made before
detailed design determine most of a project's eventual cost and schedule outcome.

## Turnarounds as a discipline {claim: historical_fact}
Refinery and petrochemical turnarounds grew into the largest single maintenance events in
industry, often thousands of workers and tens of thousands of activities over a few
weeks. Dedicated turnaround management methodology developed because normal project
practice could not handle the discovery work and compressed duration.

## Front-end loading {claim: engineering_principle}
The cheapest time to change a project is before design is fixed. Scope definition,
constructability review, and early identification of long-lead items determine outcomes
far more than execution efficiency does. Late scope change is the dominant cause of
turnaround overrun.

## Work list discipline {claim: engineering_principle}
A turnaround work list should be frozen at a defined date, with a formal challenge
process for late additions. Uncontrolled growth of the work list - additions after
freeze, and discovery work found on opening equipment - is what converts a planned
duration into an overrun.

## Critical path thinking {claim: engineering_principle}
Only activities on the critical path determine the end date. Resources spent accelerating
non-critical work buy nothing. Identifying the true critical path - usually a small
number of major vessels, exchangers or heaters - is the first step in any credible
turnaround plan.

## Discovery work {claim: engineering_principle}
Inspection on opening always finds unexpected condition. Mature turnaround plans budget
contingency for discovery, pre-stage likely spares, and have a decision process ready,
rather than treating discovery as an exception.

## Interfaces and permits {claim: engineering_principle}
On a large turnaround the binding constraint is often not craft labour but permits,
isolation, scaffolding, crane availability and simultaneous-operations conflict.
Planning these interfaces is what separates a schedule that can be executed from one
that merely balances.

## Pre-startup safety review {claim: engineering_principle}
Before any unit restarts after a turnaround or modification, PSSR confirms that
equipment is as designed, procedures are updated, protective systems are in service, and
training has been done. It is the last barrier before hazardous material is reintroduced.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice uses milestone-based turnaround readiness reviews, scope freeze
gates, integrated planning with inspection findings from the previous cycle, contractor
pre-qualification and mobilisation planning, and post-turnaround review that feeds the
next cycle's scope.
