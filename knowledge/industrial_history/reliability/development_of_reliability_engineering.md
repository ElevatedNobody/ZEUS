title: Development of Reliability Engineering

## Origins in electronics and aerospace {claim: historical_fact}
Reliability engineering emerged from wartime and post-war experience with electronic
equipment that failed far more often in the field than in test. United States military
programmes in the 1950s produced the first formal reliability prediction and
demonstration methods.

## Weibull analysis {claim: historical_fact}
Waloddi Weibull published his distribution paper in 1951. Its flexibility - one
distribution family covering infant mortality, random and wear-out behaviour through a
single shape parameter - made it the standard tool for analysing life data from a small
number of failures, which is the normal industrial situation.

## FMEA and FMECA {claim: historical_fact}
Failure mode and effects analysis was developed by the United States military in the
1940s and 1950s and adopted by NASA for the Apollo programme and later by the
automotive industry. It works bottom-up: for each component, what can fail, how, with
what effect, and how would it be detected.

## Fault tree analysis {claim: historical_fact}
Fault tree analysis was developed at Bell Laboratories in 1962 for the Minuteman
missile programme and works top-down from an undesired event to combinations of causes.
It became central to quantitative risk assessment in nuclear and process industries.

## Reliability centred maintenance {claim: historical_fact}
RCM formalised the aviation Maintenance Steering Group logic (MSG-1 for the Boeing 747,
then MSG-2 and MSG-3) into a general method, published for industry through the Nowlan
and Heap report and later standardised as SAE JA1011 in 1999.

## Asset management standards {claim: historical_fact}
PAS 55 was published in the United Kingdom in 2004 and became the basis for the ISO
55000 series on asset management in 2014, placing maintenance and reliability inside a
whole-life value framework rather than treating them as a cost centre.

## Reliability, availability, maintainability {claim: engineering_principle}
Reliability is the probability of running without failure for a period; maintainability
is how quickly function is restored; availability combines both. An asset with poor
reliability but excellent maintainability can still deliver high availability - which is
why sparing and repair strategy matter as much as failure rate.

## The bathtub curve and its limits {claim: engineering_principle}
The classic bathtub curve - infant mortality, useful life, wear-out - describes only a
minority of industrial failure modes. Assuming it universally leads to overhaul
schedules that induce more failures than they prevent.

## Bad actor analysis {claim: engineering_principle}
A small proportion of equipment usually causes the majority of unplanned downtime.
Ranking equipment by cumulative consequence, then eliminating defects on the top few,
gives a far better return than uniform effort across the plant.

## Root cause analysis {claim: engineering_principle}
Effective RCA distinguishes the physical root (what broke and why, mechanically), the
human root (what action or omission contributed) and the latent or systemic root (what
in the management system allowed it). Stopping at the physical root guarantees
recurrence.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Reliability work today typically includes criticality analysis, Weibull analysis of
failure history from the CMMS, defect elimination programmes, spare-parts optimisation
based on failure rate and lead time, and reliability acceptance criteria written into
purchase specifications for new equipment.
