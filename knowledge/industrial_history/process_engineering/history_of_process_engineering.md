## The unit operations idea {claim: historical_fact}
Chemical engineering became a distinct discipline when Arthur D. Little articulated
"unit operations" in 1915: any chemical plant is a sequence of a limited set of
physical operations - distillation, absorption, heat transfer, filtration, drying -
combined with reaction. This made plant design teachable and transferable between
industries.

## Transport phenomena {claim: historical_fact}
The publication of Bird, Stewart and Lightfoot's Transport Phenomena in 1960 unified
momentum, heat and mass transfer on a common mathematical basis and moved design from
empirical correlation toward first principles.

## Process simulation {claim: historical_fact}
Computer flowsheet simulation grew from the 1960s; the ASPEN project at MIT ran from
1976 and led to commercial simulators that are now standard for design, revamp and
operator training.

## Process integration {claim: historical_fact}
Pinch analysis, developed by Bodo Linnhoff and co-workers from the late 1970s, provided
a systematic way to set energy targets for a heat exchanger network before designing it.
Refinery crude preheat trains were among its first large industrial applications.

## Mass and energy balance {claim: engineering_principle}
Every process question ultimately reduces to a balance: what enters, what leaves, what
accumulates, and what is generated or consumed. Most plant data problems reveal
themselves as a balance that does not close, which is why reconciliation is the first
step in any credible performance study.

## Distillation fundamentals {claim: engineering_principle}
Separation by distillation depends on relative volatility, the number of theoretical
stages and the reflux ratio. There is a minimum reflux below which separation is
impossible at any number of stages, and a minimum number of stages at total reflux.
Real columns trade energy (reflux) against capital (trays or packing).

## Heat transfer fundamentals {claim: engineering_principle}
Duty equals overall coefficient times area times the corrected log mean temperature
difference. Fouling reduces the overall coefficient over time, which appears in
operation as a rising temperature approach and falling duty long before anything fails.

## Fluid flow and hydraulics {claim: engineering_principle}
Pressure drop, net positive suction head, two-phase flow regime and control valve
authority determine whether a design actually works at turndown. A large fraction of
so-called equipment problems are hydraulic design problems that only appear off design.

## Reaction engineering {claim: engineering_principle}
Conversion, selectivity and yield are distinct: high conversion at poor selectivity can
be worse economically than moderate conversion. Catalyst deactivation - coking, poison,
sintering - sets run length and is the usual reason a unit is shut down.

## Process safety in design {claim: engineering_principle}
Inherently safer design, articulated by Trevor Kletz, prefers eliminating a hazard to
controlling it: minimise inventory, substitute a less hazardous material, moderate
conditions, and simplify. Kletz's formulation - what you do not have cannot leak -
remains the most useful single sentence in process safety.

## Current practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Modern process engineering combines steady-state and dynamic simulation, operator
training simulators, data reconciliation against plant historians, and model predictive
control on the units where constraints bind.
