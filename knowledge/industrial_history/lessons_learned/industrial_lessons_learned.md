## How to use this file {claim: engineering_principle}
These are generalised lessons drawn from published industry investigations. They are
engineering guidance, not findings about any specific plant. When ZEUS uses them, they
must be presented as industry practice, never as a conclusion read from a user's own
inspection or maintenance record.

## Change is the most dangerous activity {claim: engineering_principle}
Flixborough, and many smaller events since, show that modification outside a controlled
process is the single highest-risk activity in an operating plant. Temporary
installations, "like-for-like" replacements that are not, and software changes to
protective systems all require the same assessment as a permanent design change.

## Protection layers degrade silently {claim: engineering_principle}
Relief valves stuck, trips inhibited, detectors out of service, alarms disabled -
degradation of a protection layer produces no immediate consequence, so it accumulates
until an initiating event arrives. Tracking safety-critical elements out of service is
therefore a leading indicator of real risk.

## Handover is a failure point {claim: engineering_principle}
Piper Alpha turned on a shift handover that did not carry a critical fact. Structured
handover - written, specific, covering isolations, incomplete work and abnormal
conditions - is a barrier in its own right.

## Start-up and shutdown are the high-risk periods {claim: engineering_principle}
A disproportionate share of major incidents occur during start-up, shutdown or other
transient operation, when normal control assumptions do not hold and instrumentation may
be out of range. Procedures and staffing for transients deserve more attention than
steady-state operation, not less.

## Alarm floods defeat operators {claim: engineering_principle}
When an upset produces hundreds of alarms in minutes, the operator cannot triage them.
Alarm rationalisation, prioritisation and suppression of known consequential alarms are
required for the alarm system to function as a protection layer at all.

## Small-bore and dead legs leak {claim: engineering_principle}
A large share of hydrocarbon releases originate from small-bore connections, drains,
vents and dead legs that were outside routine inspection scope. Inspection coverage
should be driven by leak history, not by pipe size.

## Corrosion under insulation hides {claim: engineering_principle}
CUI is invisible during external inspection and has caused numerous releases from lines
that looked sound. Targeted insulation removal at supports, penetrations, low points and
in the wet temperature range is the practical control.

## Deferred work accumulates risk {claim: engineering_principle}
Individually reasonable deferrals of inspection, proof test or corrective work combine
into a materially different risk profile. Deferral should require the same rigour of
assessment as the original decision to do the work.

## Contractors need the same standard {claim: engineering_principle}
Many serious injuries and several major incidents involve contract staff working to a
different standard of supervision, competence assurance or permit discipline than
employees. Barrier quality cannot depend on who employs the person performing the task.

## Learning must change something {claim: modern_best_practice}
An incident investigation that generates recommendations without owners, dates and
verification of effectiveness produces no improvement. The measurable test of a
lessons-learned system is whether repeat events decline, not whether reports are filed.
