## Standardisation of pipe {claim: historical_fact}
Pipe sizing and threading were standardised in the nineteenth century, culminating in
the nominal pipe size and schedule system still used today, where the outside diameter
is fixed for a nominal size and the schedule number sets the wall thickness.

## The piping codes {claim: historical_fact}
ASME B31 grew from a 1935 American Standard for pressure piping into a family of codes.
B31.3 covers process piping in refineries and chemical plants; B31.4 and B31.8 cover
liquid and gas transportation pipelines; B31.1 covers power piping. In-service piping
inspection in refineries follows API 570.

## Welding qualification {claim: historical_fact}
As welded construction replaced threaded and flanged joints for permanent service, the
qualification of procedures and welders under ASME Section IX became the mechanism by
which piping integrity is assured before a system is ever pressurised.

## Flexibility and stress analysis {claim: engineering_principle}
Piping must absorb thermal expansion without overloading equipment nozzles. Expansion
loops, spring hangers and correct anchoring exist for that reason. A pipe that cannot
move where the designer intended will impose its load somewhere else - usually on a
pump or vessel nozzle.

## Supports are safety-critical {claim: engineering_principle}
Failed, corroded or missing supports are among the most common findings on external
piping inspection. A pipe resting on a corroded support traps water at the contact
point, which is one of the classic locations for corrosion under insulation and for
localised external wall loss.

## Dead legs and injection points {claim: engineering_principle}
Dead legs accumulate water, sediment and corrosive species and corrode faster than the
live line. Injection and mixing points suffer accelerated attack downstream from
turbulence and chemistry change. Both are specifically called out in inspection codes as
locations requiring dedicated CMLs.

## Small-bore connections {claim: engineering_principle}
Small-bore branch connections, instrument tappings and vent and drain lines fail from
vibration-induced fatigue far more often than main lines, and they are frequently
omitted from inspection scope. A disproportionate number of hydrocarbon leaks originate
from them.

## Erosion and velocity limits {claim: engineering_principle}
Velocity limits exist to prevent erosion-corrosion, particularly in two-phase and
sand-bearing flow, and to keep noise and vibration within acceptable limits. Revamps
that increase throughput through existing lines routinely violate the original velocity
assumptions.

## Isometrics, line lists and change control {claim: modern_best_practice}
Piping integrity depends on documentation: an accurate line list with service, design
conditions and material; isometrics that match the field; and a CML register keyed to
both. Undocumented modification - the direct cause of the Flixborough disaster - remains
the most dangerous piping practice.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice covers piping through corrosion loops rather than line by line, uses
risk-based inspection to set intervals, targets known-vulnerable locations (dead legs,
injection points, supports, small bore), and requires vibration assessment for lines
subject to flow-induced or machine-induced excitation.
