## Shell and tube {claim: historical_fact}
The shell and tube exchanger became the industrial standard because it is robust, wide
in pressure and temperature range, and mechanically cleanable. The Tubular Exchanger
Manufacturers Association, formed in 1939, published the TEMA standards whose
three-letter type designations describe front head, shell and rear head construction.

## Plate exchangers {claim: historical_fact}
The gasketed plate heat exchanger was developed for the dairy industry in the 1920s by
APV under Richard Seligman and later spread to chemical and refinery duty, offering high
surface density and close temperature approach where pressure and gasket compatibility
allow.

## Air coolers and specialised types {claim: historical_fact}
Air-cooled exchangers spread where cooling water is scarce. Spiral, welded plate,
printed circuit and compact exchangers occupy niches where fouling, approach temperature
or space dominate the choice.

## Heat integration {claim: historical_fact}
Pinch analysis from the late 1970s turned exchanger networks from ad hoc arrangements
into designed systems with an energy target. Crude preheat trains are the classic
industrial application and typically the largest single energy saving available in a
refinery.

## The design equation {claim: engineering_principle}
Duty equals overall heat transfer coefficient times area times corrected log mean
temperature difference. The overall coefficient combines film coefficients on both
sides, tube wall conduction and fouling resistances; whichever resistance dominates is
the one worth improving.

## Fouling {claim: engineering_principle}
Fouling grows with time and reduces the overall coefficient. In operation it shows up as
a rising temperature approach, falling duty and rising pressure drop - a measurable trend
long before any operating limit is reached. Crude preheat fouling directly costs fired
heater fuel and can constrain throughput.

## Cleaning and its economics {claim: engineering_principle}
The decision when to clean an exchanger is an optimisation: the fuel and throughput cost
of continued fouling against the cost and risk of taking it offline. Monitoring the
fouling factor trend allows that decision to be made from data rather than from a fixed
schedule.

## Mechanical failure modes {claim: engineering_principle}
Tube-to-tubesheet joint leaks, tube vibration damage in the baffle window, thermal
fatigue from rapid temperature swings, under-deposit corrosion, and erosion at the tube
inlet are the recurring mechanical problems. Tube rupture is a defined overpressure
scenario for the low-pressure side and must appear in the relief study.

## Water-side integrity {claim: engineering_principle}
Cooling water side failures - microbiologically influenced corrosion, scaling, and
chloride pitting of stainless tubes - are controlled by water chemistry, velocity limits
and biocide dosing rather than by exchanger design.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice tracks each significant exchanger's fouling factor from routine process
data, applies eddy current or IRIS inspection to tube bundles at turnaround, and uses
online or chemical cleaning where mechanical cleaning is impractical.
