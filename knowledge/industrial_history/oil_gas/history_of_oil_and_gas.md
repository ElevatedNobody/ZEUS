## Pre-industrial use of petroleum {claim: historical_fact}
Natural seeps of bitumen and asphalt were used for waterproofing, mortar and medicine
in Mesopotamia, Persia, China and the Americas for thousands of years. Chinese drillers
reached considerable depths with percussion cable tools using bamboo pipe well before
European drilling.

## The birth of the modern industry {claim: historical_fact}
Edwin Drake's well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, struck oil at about 69 feet on 27
August 1859. Its significance is not the depth but the method: purpose-drilled wells
with driven casing turned oil from a scavenged curiosity into a produced commodity.
Kerosene for lighting was the first mass market.

## Integration and its breakup {claim: historical_fact}
Standard Oil, founded by John D. Rockefeller in 1870, integrated refining, transport
and distribution and at its peak controlled most United States refining. The Supreme
Court ordered its dissolution in 1911; several of today's major companies descend from
the fragments.

## Spindletop and the age of gasoline {claim: historical_fact}
The Spindletop gusher near Beaumont, Texas, in January 1901 produced at an
unprecedented rate and shifted the industry's centre of gravity to Texas. The rise of
the automobile changed the primary product from kerosene to gasoline.

## Middle East discoveries {claim: historical_fact}
Commercial oil was found in Persia in 1908, in Iraq at Kirkuk in 1927, in Bahrain in
1932, and in Saudi Arabia at Dammam No. 7 in 1938. These discoveries reshaped both
global energy supply and international politics for the rest of the century.

## Producer organisation and price shocks {claim: historical_fact}
OPEC was founded in Baghdad in September 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and
Venezuela. The 1973 embargo and the 1979 disruption produced sustained high prices,
which in turn funded North Sea and Alaskan development and drove the first serious
energy-efficiency programmes in refining.

## Offshore development {claim: historical_fact}
Production moved from marsh and shallow water to fixed steel platforms in the Gulf of
Mexico from the late 1940s, to the harsh-environment North Sea from the 1970s, and to
deepwater and subsea tiebacks from the 1990s. Each step increased the consequence of
failure and drove formal safety-case regulation.

## Natural gas as a business in its own right {claim: historical_fact}
Gas was long flared as a nuisance. Long-distance welded pipelines from the 1930s and
1940s, and LNG shipping from the 1960s, made gas tradeable. Gas processing - removing
acid gas, water and heavier hydrocarbons - became an industry of its own.

## Unconventional resources {claim: historical_fact}
The combination of horizontal drilling and multi-stage hydraulic fracturing made shale
gas and tight oil commercial in the United States from the mid-2000s, changing global
supply balances within a decade.

## The value chain {claim: engineering_principle}
Upstream finds and produces; midstream transports, stores and processes; downstream
refines and markets. Equipment reliability requirements differ sharply along the chain:
upstream is dominated by remote unmanned operation and well integrity, midstream by
pipeline integrity and leak detection, downstream by fired equipment, rotating machinery
and containment of hot hydrocarbon.

## Current direction {claim: modern_best_practice}
Operators now plan around emissions intensity, methane leak detection and repair,
energy efficiency of existing assets, and extending asset life through integrity
management rather than new build.
