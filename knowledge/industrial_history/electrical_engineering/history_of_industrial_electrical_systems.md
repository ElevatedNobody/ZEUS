## From steam to electric drive {claim: historical_fact}
Industrial electrification began in the 1880s. The decisive change was not lighting but
the electric motor: replacing line shafts with individual drives freed factory layout
and allowed each machine to be started, stopped and protected independently.

## Alternating current and the grid {claim: historical_fact}
The competition between direct and alternating current in the 1880s and 1890s was
settled by transformers, which made high-voltage transmission and therefore large
central generating stations practical. Industrial plants moved from private generation
to grid supply with local distribution.

## Protection and switchgear {claim: historical_fact}
Fuses, then oil circuit breakers, then air, vacuum and SF6 breakers, and finally
microprocessor-based protective relays progressively reduced the time to clear a fault
from seconds to a few cycles. Fast clearing is what makes a fault survivable for
connected equipment.

## Hazardous area electrical practice {claim: historical_fact}
Flameproof and intrinsically safe equipment developed from mining practice - the Davy
lamp of 1815 is the ancestor - and was formalised in twentieth-century area
classification systems that divide plants into zones by likelihood of a flammable
atmosphere.

## Variable speed drives {claim: historical_fact}
Power electronics from the 1960s onward, and cheap insulated-gate bipolar transistors
from the 1990s, made variable frequency drives standard. Throttling a pump with a valve
wastes energy; varying speed does not, which made VFDs one of the most reliable energy
savings in process plants.

## Motors dominate industrial electrical load {claim: engineering_principle}
In a typical process plant, electric motors consume the large majority of electrical
energy, and pumps, fans and compressors dominate that motor load. Electrical
reliability work concentrates where that load is: motor insulation condition, bearing
currents, starting duty and supply quality.

## Earthing, bonding and static {claim: engineering_principle}
Loss of containment incidents have been initiated by static discharge during tank
filling and non-conductive hose use. Bonding and earthing are not electrical
housekeeping; in flammable service they are a process safety barrier.

## Condition monitoring of electrical assets {claim: modern_best_practice}
Insulation resistance and polarisation index, partial discharge measurement, infrared
thermography of terminations, and motor current signature analysis are the standard
techniques for detecting electrical degradation before failure.

## Power system studies {claim: modern_best_practice}
Load flow, short-circuit, protection coordination and arc-flash studies should be
revalidated after any significant change to the plant's electrical configuration; an
outdated coordination study is a common finding in electrical audits.

## Reliability of supply {claim: engineering_principle}
Process plants tolerate voltage dips poorly: a dip of a few cycles can trip contactors
and drives and shut down a whole unit even though nothing was damaged. Ride-through
capability, UPS scope and the design of critical loads are therefore process
availability questions, not just electrical ones.
