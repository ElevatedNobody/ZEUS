## Reciprocating machines first {claim: historical_fact}
Industrial gas compression began with reciprocating machines driven by steam and later
by gas and electric motors. They remain the choice for high pressure ratio and moderate
flow, and dominate hydrogen make-up service in refineries.

## Centrifugal compression {claim: historical_fact}
Auguste Rateau developed multistage centrifugal compressors around the turn of the
twentieth century. Their high flow capacity, absence of reciprocating masses and long
run length made them standard for large process duty as plant scale grew.

## Screw compressors {claim: historical_fact}
The oil-flooded twin-screw compressor, developed on the basis of Alf Lysholm's work in
the 1930s, filled the middle ground: continuous flow like a centrifugal machine, but
positive displacement behaviour and tolerance of varying molecular weight.

## Sealing and standards {claim: historical_fact}
Dry gas seals largely replaced oil film seals on centrifugal compressors from the 1970s
and 1980s, removing seal oil systems and their contamination problems. API 617, 618 and
619 standardise centrifugal, reciprocating and rotary compressors for petroleum service.

## Compression fundamentals {claim: engineering_principle}
Discharge temperature rises with pressure ratio and depends on the gas properties;
intercooling between stages limits it. Centrifugal machine head depends on gas molecular
weight, which is why a change in gas composition changes the machine's delivered
pressure even at constant speed.

## Surge {claim: engineering_principle}
Every dynamic compressor has a minimum stable flow at each speed. Below it, flow
reverses cyclically - surge - imposing thrust reversals and thermal cycling that can
destroy a machine in seconds. Anti-surge protection uses a fast recycle valve whose
speed of response, not merely its presence, determines protection.

## Reciprocating compressor specifics {claim: engineering_principle}
Valve failure is the dominant maintenance item on reciprocating compressors. Capacity
control by unloaders, pulsation control by dampeners and correct piping design under API
618 acoustic study are what keep pulsation-induced vibration and small-bore fatigue
failures under control.

## Lube and seal systems {claim: engineering_principle}
Auxiliary systems cause a large share of compressor trips: lube oil pressure, filter
differential, seal gas supply pressure and purity, and cooler performance. Reliability
work on compressors usually pays better on the auxiliaries than on the machine itself.

## Monitoring {claim: modern_best_practice}
Critical compressors carry API 670 machinery protection: radial and axial proximity
probes, key phasor, bearing temperatures, and vibration monitoring feeding both
protection and a condition monitoring system. Performance monitoring - polytropic
efficiency against the reference curve - detects fouling and internal wear that vibration
does not.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice includes anti-surge controller response testing, dry gas seal condition
monitoring by leakage flow trending, gas composition tracking for machines whose duty
changes, and run-length management tied to a planned turnaround rather than to failure.
