## Riveted to welded construction {claim: historical_fact}
Early pressure vessels and tanks were riveted. Welded construction, adopted widely from
the 1930s and 1940s, allowed larger, cleaner and cheaper equipment, but transferred the
integrity question from joint design to weld quality and material toughness - which is
why radiography and post-weld heat treatment became code requirements.

## The pressure vessel codes {claim: historical_fact}
The ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, first issued in 1914-1915, and later Section
VIII for pressure vessels, established design by rule with defined allowable stresses,
joint efficiencies and inspection requirements. Other jurisdictions developed parallel
codes, and India's Indian Boiler Regulations of 1950 govern boilers and certain
associated pressure parts.

## Storage tanks {claim: historical_fact}
API 650 became the dominant standard for welded aboveground storage tanks and API 653
for their inspection, repair and reconstruction. Floating roofs, introduced to reduce
evaporation losses and vapour space explosions, brought their own maintenance issues -
seal wear, roof drainage and roof landing.

## Distillation columns {claim: historical_fact}
Bubble cap trays gave way to sieve and valve trays, and later to structured packing from
the 1970s, which offered lower pressure drop and more theoretical stages per metre. Many
refinery revamps have been achieved by replacing internals rather than the shell.

## Design by rule and design by analysis {claim: engineering_principle}
Code design by rule uses formulas with built-in margin. Design by analysis, using finite
element methods, is used for geometries the rules do not cover, and for fitness for
service assessments of equipment that already has flaws - most commonly through API 579.

## Maximum allowable working pressure {claim: engineering_principle}
MAWP is determined by the weakest pressure-containing part at operating temperature and
sets the relief valve set pressure. As corrosion consumes wall thickness, MAWP falls;
inspection therefore does not merely record thickness, it revalidates the pressure the
equipment is allowed to hold.

## Relief and overpressure protection {claim: engineering_principle}
Every credible overpressure scenario - blocked outlet, fire case, control valve failure,
tube rupture, thermal expansion - must have a protection path. Relief system adequacy is
a design calculation that must be revisited whenever the process changes, which is one
of the most commonly missed items in management of change.

## Nozzles, supports and cyclic service {claim: engineering_principle}
Failures in static equipment concentrate at discontinuities: nozzle-to-shell junctions,
support attachments, and locations with thermal gradients. Equipment in cyclic thermal
or pressure service needs fatigue consideration that steady-service equipment does not.

## Fitness for service {claim: modern_best_practice}
API 579 / ASME FFS-1 provides staged assessment levels for equipment with metal loss,
pitting, blisters, cracks, creep damage or distortion, allowing a documented,
engineering-based decision to continue running, to derate, or to repair - instead of a
binary scrap-or-ignore choice.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice for static equipment combines corrosion loop definition, risk-based
inspection intervals, CML registers with trended thickness, relief system revalidation
on change, and fitness for service assessment where measured condition falls below the
original design assumption.
