## Visual inspection and hydrostatic testing {claim: historical_fact}
The earliest systematic inspection of pressure equipment was visual examination plus
hydrostatic proof testing, introduced by boiler insurers and inspection bodies in the
nineteenth century after repeated boiler explosions. Proof testing demonstrates
integrity at a moment in time but does not detect a slowly growing flaw.

## Radiography {claim: historical_fact}
Industrial radiography developed rapidly after the First World War and became a
standard weld examination method. It gave, for the first time, a permanent record of
what was inside a joint.

## Ultrasonic testing {claim: historical_fact}
Pulse-echo ultrasonic testing was developed in the late 1930s and 1940s and became the
workhorse of thickness measurement and flaw detection. Modern variants - phased array
and time-of-flight diffraction - give better sizing and imaging of flaws.

## Magnetic particle and dye penetrant {claim: historical_fact}
Surface-breaking crack detection by magnetic particle inspection and by liquid
penetrant became standard in the mid twentieth century and remain the cheapest reliable
methods for surface flaws on ferritic and non-ferritic materials respectively.

## Codes for in-service inspection {claim: historical_fact}
The American Petroleum Institute published inspection codes that became the global
reference for refineries: API 510 for pressure vessels, API 570 for piping (first
published 1993) and API 653 for aboveground storage tanks (first published 1991). They
define inspection intervals, thickness calculation and repair rules for equipment in
service, as distinct from construction codes.

## Risk-based inspection {claim: historical_fact}
API 580 and the quantitative methodology of API 581, published from around 2000, moved
inspection planning from fixed intervals to risk - the product of likelihood of failure
from damage mechanisms and consequence of failure. Effort is concentrated where risk
justifies it.

## Damage mechanism knowledge {claim: historical_fact}
API 571, first issued in 2003, catalogued the damage mechanisms that actually occur in
refineries - sulphidation, naphthenic acid corrosion, high temperature hydrogen attack,
chloride stress corrosion cracking, creep, amine cracking and others - with their
appearance, affected materials and inspection methods.

## The inspection logic {claim: engineering_principle}
Credible inspection requires four things in order: which damage mechanisms are
plausible for this material in this service; where they act; which technique can detect
them; and how often, given the expected rate. An inspection plan that skips the damage
mechanism step measures thickness in the wrong places.

## Condition monitoring locations {claim: engineering_principle}
Thickness readings are meaningful only when taken repeatedly at the same defined
locations. Corrosion rate is calculated from the change between readings at a fixed CML,
and remaining life from the margin above minimum required thickness divided by that
rate. Scattered readings at arbitrary points cannot support a rate calculation.

## Integrity operating windows {claim: engineering_principle}
IOWs define the process limits - temperature, water chemistry, sulphur, chloride,
velocity - within which the assumed corrosion rates hold. Operating outside an IOW
invalidates the inspection plan, which is why exceedances must be reported to the
inspection engineer rather than treated only as an operating deviation.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current inspection practice combines risk-based intervals, permanently installed
thickness sensors on inaccessible or high-rate locations, drone and crawler external
survey, digital capture of readings against a CML register, and corrosion loop
management that groups equipment by shared service and mechanism.
