## Why standards exist {claim: historical_fact}
Standardisation began with interchangeability - Whitworth threads in 1841, standard pipe
sizes, standard flange dimensions - and expanded into design rules after accidents made
clear that individual judgement was not sufficient protection. Almost every major
standard family can be traced to a category of failure.

## The main standards bodies {claim: historical_fact}
ASME publishes the Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code and the B31 piping codes. API
publishes upstream and downstream standards including the 500-series inspection codes and
the 600-series machinery standards. ISO and IEC publish international standards, IEC
61511 for safety instrumented systems among them. ISA publishes instrumentation and
automation standards. In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards publishes IS standards,
and OISD publishes oil-industry-specific standards.

## Construction codes versus in-service codes {claim: engineering_principle}
A construction code such as ASME VIII governs how equipment is designed and built. An
in-service code such as API 510, 570 or 653 governs how it is inspected, evaluated,
repaired and re-rated afterwards. Applying a construction code to in-service equipment,
or the reverse, is a recurring source of incorrect decisions.

## Standards, codes and recommended practices {claim: engineering_principle}
A code is often adopted into law and is mandatory where adopted; a standard is a
consensus technical requirement; a recommended practice is guidance. The distinction
matters in an inspection finding: a deviation from a mandatory code is a compliance
issue, a deviation from a recommended practice is an engineering judgement.

## Engineering documentation {claim: historical_fact}
Drawing practice evolved from hand-drawn linen through blueprints and microfilm to CAD
from the 1980s and to 3D plant models and data-centric handover from the 2000s. The
P&ID emerged as the single most important operating document because it links equipment,
piping, instrumentation and control on one sheet.

## Document control {claim: engineering_principle}
A document without a revision and an approval status is not usable for a safety
decision. Superseded drawings that remain in circulation have contributed to incidents;
this is why controlled copies, as-built updating after modification and formal document
hierarchies exist.

## The as-built problem {claim: engineering_principle}
The most common documentation failure in operating plants is drift between the drawings
and the plant. Modifications executed under time pressure without drawing update leave
the operator working from a description of a plant that no longer exists - the condition
underlying several major incidents.

## Tag numbering {claim: engineering_principle}
Consistent tag numbering is the key that joins P&IDs, equipment registers, maintenance
history, inspection records and control system tags. Where tag conventions diverge
between systems, cross-referencing becomes manual and equipment history becomes
unreliable.

## Handover and data {claim: modern_best_practice}
Modern capital projects hand over structured engineering data - equipment attributes,
line lists, instrument index - as well as documents, so the maintenance and inspection
systems can be populated without re-keying from drawings.

## Modern practice {claim: modern_best_practice}
Current practice keeps a controlled master document register, requires drawing update as
a closure condition for management of change, and increasingly maintains a digital asset
model that the operating systems reference rather than copy.
