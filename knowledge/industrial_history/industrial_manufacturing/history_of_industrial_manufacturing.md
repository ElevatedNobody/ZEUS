## Craft production before mechanisation {claim: historical_fact}
Before the eighteenth century almost all goods were made one at a time by skilled
craftsmen. Parts were fitted to each other individually, so no two assemblies were
identical and a broken component had to be remade rather than replaced. Output was
limited by human muscle, water wheels and the number of trained hands available.

## The first industrial revolution {claim: historical_fact}
From roughly 1760 the combination of coke smelting, the Watt steam engine (separate
condenser patented 1769), textile machinery and canal and rail transport moved
production from workshops into factories. Power was distributed mechanically through
line shafts and belts, which is why early factories were tall, narrow and organised
around the prime mover rather than around the flow of work.

## Interchangeable parts and precision {claim: historical_fact}
The armouries of the United States in the early nineteenth century, and Whitworth's
standardisation of screw threads in Britain in 1841, established that parts made to a
tolerance could be interchanged. This is the foundation of every later development in
maintenance: a spare part only exists as a concept once parts are interchangeable.

## Electrification and the second industrial revolution {claim: historical_fact}
From the 1880s electric motors replaced line shafting. Because each machine could have
its own drive, factories could be laid out along the process rather than around the
power source. Steel, chemicals, and the internal combustion engine industries grew in
the same period.

## Mass production and the moving assembly line {claim: historical_fact}
Ford's moving assembly line at Highland Park in 1913 reduced Model T chassis assembly
from over twelve hours to about ninety minutes. Mass production traded flexibility for
throughput and made work-in-progress, line balancing and machine availability
commercially critical for the first time.

## Statistical quality control {claim: historical_fact}
Walter Shewhart introduced the control chart at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1924,
separating common-cause variation from special-cause variation. W. Edwards Deming and
Joseph Juran carried these methods to Japanese industry after 1950.

## Lean production {claim: historical_fact}
The Toyota Production System developed from the 1950s under Taiichi Ohno, built on
just-in-time flow, jidoka (stopping on a defect), levelled scheduling and reduction of
the seven wastes. The term "lean production" entered wide use after the MIT
International Motor Vehicle Programme published The Machine That Changed the World in
1990.

## The third industrial revolution: automation and computers {claim: historical_fact}
Numerically controlled machine tools (MIT, early 1950s), the programmable logic
controller (1969) and computer aided design turned control of production over to
digital systems. Continuous process industries such as refining were early adopters
because their processes ran without interruption and benefited most from stable
automatic control.

## The fourth industrial revolution {claim: historical_fact}
"Industrie 4.0" was launched as a German national initiative and publicised at the
Hannover Messe in 2011. It describes cyber-physical production systems, pervasive
sensing, and the integration of operational technology with information technology.

## What actually transfers between eras {claim: engineering_principle}
Every step above solved a bottleneck and created a new one: power, then precision,
then throughput, then quality, then flexibility, then information. Understanding which
bottleneck a plant is actually against matters more than the technology label attached
to a proposal.

## Manufacturing today {claim: modern_best_practice}
Modern plants combine levelled flow, condition-based maintenance, digital work
instructions, and data captured at the point of work. The common failure mode is
collecting data without changing any decision, which produces cost without benefit.
